Why this site exists

Is the fentanyl crisis getting better or worse?

Loading the latest month…

Checking how current the CDC files are…

This site was built because that question was surprisingly hard to answer. The deaths are public, but they are split across CDC files. Recent months are still preliminary. There was no plain dashboard that said whether the toll was rising or falling, explained what fentanyl is, and handed an analyst a file they could trust.

It is also here because the loss is enormous, and easy to lose sight of. These counts are people. Putting them in one place is a way to keep that visible, and to make it easier to see whether anything is changing.

  1. See the trend. Finished death certificates are used for older months. CDC's preliminary counts continue the series after the last final year, instead of stopping there.
  2. Learn what fentanyl is. Pharmaceutical fentanyl and illicit fentanyl are not the same thing, and the death category CDC publishes is wider than fentanyl alone.
  3. Look at public policy. Border, immigration, supply-chain, and China actions are marked on the death series. A mark is a date, not proof the action worked.
  4. Download the dataset. One file by month, state, and year. It is refreshed every Monday when CDC has posted a new month.

What fentanyl is

Fentanyl is a synthetic opioid. In medicine, pharmaceutical fentanyl is used for severe pain. The deaths in this crisis are tied to illicit fentanyl, which is often pressed into counterfeit pills or mixed into other drugs. A very small amount can be fatal. DEA's fentanyl fact sheet describes that difference.

The counts here are not a fentanyl-only number. CDC publishes them as synthetic opioids, excluding methadone. That group includes fentanyl and other synthetics such as tramadol. CDC does not publish a public series of fentanyl use, so this site answers the question with deaths. CDC's overdose page explains why recent months are still preliminary.

The series does not stop at the last finished year. Older months stay on the death certificate. Later months use CDC's preliminary 12-month totals, labeled as preliminary, and the national chart turns that change into an estimate for the month. State rows in the download keep the preliminary 12-month total and do not invent a monthly count.
